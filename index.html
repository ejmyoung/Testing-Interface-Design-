What is a Test, Really?

All of us have at least one experience in lifetime of taking tests, and have some idea of what a test is, but let's dig deeper.
So — what is a test?

1Let's Start With an Analogy

Imagine you are a scientist studying life in the Pacific Ocean. The water teems with creatures — fish, starfish, squid, and many more. But you are interested in only one thing: the kinds of fish people can actually eat.

You want to understand this "edible fish" population, but you cannot observe every single fish in the ocean. So you do what any good scientist does: you cast your net and catch a sample. The fish you catch give you a window into what the whole ocean holds.

Testing works exactly the same way. Watch the water on the right — then click Cast Net to see what you catch →

2Mapping the Analogy to Testing

In the Pacific Ocean, you are surrounded by many kinds of sea life — fish, starfish, squid, and more. But as a scientist, you care about only one specific group: the edible fish species people can actually eat. That is your target concept.

In testing, we do something similar. We start with a broad domain of knowledge, but we focus on a specific construct — the concept or characteristic that the test is designed to measure. Then we draw a limited sample of items (like one cast of the net) and use students' scores to reason about the larger domain.

3From Domain to Test

A domain is the full body of what a student could possibly know about a subject. Because we can never test all of it, we sample — drawing a small, representative set of items meant to stand in for the whole domain.

🎯 We Infer! We make inference about the larger domain that is not tested. The test score becomes a window into unmeasured knowledge.

Here is where Validity matters:

"Validity refers to the degree to which evidence and theory support the interpretation of test scores for proposed uses of the test."

4Threats to the Interpretability of Obtained Scores

Every test score is built from several pieces — some that reflect the construct we care about, and some that don't. Let's take it apart, piece by piece.

1. Construct
the concept or characteristic that a test is designed to measure. It is made up of two parts:

Construct = Valid Measurement + Construct Under-representation

  • Valid Measurement — the part of the construct the test successfully captures
  • Construct Under-representation — the part the test fails to capture

2. True Score
But a test can't measure the Construct directly, what it actually produces is a True Score. It is made up of:

True Score = Valid Measurement + Construct-Irrelevant Variance

  • Valid Measurement — the same "on-target" portion as before
  • Construct-Irrelevant Variance — variance from factors that have nothing to do with the construct

3. Observed Score

Finally, the actual Score a student receives adds one more piece Error on top of the True Score:

Observed Score = Error + True Score

= Error + Valid Measurement + Construct-Irrelevant Variance

  • Error — random, unsystematic noise unrelated to the construct

Takeaway

Threats to the interpretability of obtained scores are minimized by clearly defining how particular psychological tests are to be used — and by minimizing both Construct Under-representation and Construct-Irrelevant Variance.

1Meet Your Working Memory

Cognitive Load Theory is "an instructional theory based on our knowledge of human cognitive architecture which specifically addresses the limitations of working memory."

In plain terms: your brain has a limited amount of mental "workspace" available at any moment. When a student takes a computer-based test, every part of the interface pushes on that same limited workspace — not just the question itself, but every button, icon, scrollbar, and instruction around it.

Cognitive Load Theory splits that workspace into three types of load. Hover or click each one in the diagram to see what it means for test design.

Working memory is like a cup with a fixed size. Intrinsic, extraneous, and germane load are all being poured into that same cup. If the extraneous portion grows, there's less room left for everything else — including the load you actually want students spending on the construct.

2UI Complexity and Cognitive Load

Drag the UI Complexity slider from Level 1 (Simple) to Level 3 (Chaotic).
A simple user interface has an optimal amount of icons, buttons, and interactions needed to solve a given item on the test, and these interactions are consistent across items.
While a chaotic user interface has too many icons, buttons, and interactions that may distract students from solving the item, with interactions that are inconsistent across items.

Watch the Working Memory bar re-partition as you drag.

Extraneous Cognitive Load: "cognitive load imposed by nonessential, interating elements. Distracts working memory from processing new information." Poor interface design is a primary source of extraneous load in testing environments.

Notice that Extraneous Load grows the most as complexity increases, while Germane Load and Intrinsic Load shrink as we have limited working memory available to spend on the question itself.

3Why This Is a Problem in Tests

When the testing interface is unfamiliar, it adds extraneous cognitive load on top of the demands of the construct itself. If students differ in how familiar they are with that interface, their scores no longer reflect the construct alone — part of each score now reflects how comfortable that student is with the technology.

Messick (1989) describes this kind of systematic, non-construct influence on scores — regardless of its source — as construct-irrelevant variance. As the donut on the right shows, the busier the interface gets, the larger that slice grows, and the smaller the slice reflecting valid measurement becomes.

Takeaway

The more complex an interface, the more extraneous cognitive load it imposes on students. That extra load shows up as construct-irrelevant variance in their scores, which lowers the test's validity. When the interface becomes too busy, we start measuring how well students can operate the interface — not how well they know the construct.

1Meet the SBAC Plotting Interface

The Smarter Balanced Assessment Consortium (SBAC) administers year-end summative tests across the United States, serving millions of students annually.

The visualization shows a mockup of an SBAC item: students are given a table of distance (d) and time (t) values and asked to use the "Add Arrow" tool to plot two points and draw a line connecting them. To erase, students must switch to a separate "Delete" tool — clicking the line removes only the line (both points remain), while clicking a point removes that point and the line, leaving the other point behind. Fully clearing a drawing therefore takes three separate clicks.

This is the actual sample item from the Grade 8 Math Practice Test.

Question: What kinds of interaction are required to answer this item? Is there any redundancy or complication in how the tools work?

2Identify the Problems

The flowchart on the right maps out exactly what a student must click, drag, and release to answer — and to erase — this item.

1). To answer this...

  • Click the button: Add Arrow
  • Click a point in the grid → drag → release the mouse

2). To draw this... (erasing)

  • Click the button: Delete
  • Click the line
  • Click the dot(s)
Problem: To delete the drawn line, clicking the line removes only the line (both points remain), while clicking a point removes that point and the line, leaving the other point behind. Fully clearing a drawing therefore takes three separate clicks. It is redundant and time-consuming!

3). How can we redesign this?

  • To answer: click "Add Arrow" — skip it.
    Click the grid directly → drag → release .
  • To erase: click Delete once — the drawn line disappears entirely, ready to redraw. No separate line/dot clicks.
Only two interactions are required: ① click the grid, drag, and release to draw the line; ② click "Delete" to clear it and start over.

Use the toggle and "Next Step" button in the visualization to step through both versions →

3Compare: Original vs. Redesigned

Toggle between the original and the redesigned interaction. The interface looks identical — try drawing and erasing a line in each.

  • Original: select "Add Arrow," click two points, then switch to "Delete" and click the line and dot(s) separately — three clicks to fully clear
  • Redesigned: click, drag, and release anywhere on the grid to draw the line directly — no "Add Arrow" step needed
  • Redesigned: click "Delete" once and the entire line disappears, ready to redraw
Same interface, same question — but removing the redundant tool-switching steps removes construct-irrelevant friction from the task.

Final Remarks

Let's design the testing interface more carefully!

A test score is only meaningful if it can be interpreted as evidence of the construct it claims to measure — not as evidence of how well a student navigated the interface. Throughout this explorable, we saw how interface friction — extraneous tools, ambiguous icons, redundant steps — consumes working memory that should be spent on the actual task, introducing construct-irrelevant variance into the score.

Key Takeaways

1. Validity is the goal. A test is valid when its scores can be interpreted as evidence of the construct it's meant to measure — and nothing else.

2. Cognitive load is finite. Every bit of working memory spent decoding the interface is memory not spent demonstrating knowledge.

3. Small redesigns matter. In the SBAC case study, removing one redundant tool-switching step measurably reduced the interaction burden — without changing the question at all.
